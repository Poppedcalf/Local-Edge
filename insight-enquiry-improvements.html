Five Website Improvements That Can Make It Easier for Customers to Enquire

A visitor tapping a button is not the same as your business receiving, answering and winning an enquiry. Here is what a website can improve, and what it cannot.

It is easy to measure how many people looked at a page, or tapped a contact button. It is harder, and more useful, to know how many enquiries your business actually received, answered and turned into work. Improving a website helps with some of that journey and not all of it.

A click is not an enquiry

Think of the path from interest to a customer as a series of steps:

  1. A visitor reaches your page.
  2. They understand what you offer.
  3. They tap a contact option.
  4. A message is actually sent.
  5. You notice it.
  6. You reply with a useful answer.
  7. The enquiry turns out to be a fit: right place, dates, budget.
  8. The customer decides.

A website mainly influences the first four steps, and can help with the fifth if forms and links are set up properly. The last three depend on how your business operates, your prices and your reputation. That is why this article talks about making enquiring easier, not about guaranteeing results. If you count button taps and call it conversion, you may be measuring the easy part.

1. Say what you offer, for whom and where, on the first screen

Visitors decide quickly whether a page is relevant. A first screen that names what you offer, who it is for and where you are saves them guessing. Vague welcome text makes them work.

Illustration only: fictional wording, not a real customer's result

Before

“Welcome to our website. We are passionate about excellence and customer satisfaction.”

Contact link hidden in the footer.

After

“Self-catering lakeside apartments for 2 to 8 guests in Kariba. Message us with your dates.”

Message and call buttons under the heading.

2. Put contact options where a thumb can reach them

On a phone, a contact option should be one tap away, and it should do something useful.

  • Make phone numbers tappable (tel: links) so a number is not just text.
  • Use a WhatsApp link with a message already started. The format is wa.me/<number>?text=<message>, with the number in full international format. You can use a different pre-filled message for each page or button, so you know what the person is asking about.
  • Never put your number only inside an image, because it cannot be tapped.
  • If you use a floating button, make sure it does not cover text or the buttons of a form.

Illustration only: fictional wording

Before

A phone number shown as part of a banner image. Visitors must memorise it or type it out.

After

A “Message us on WhatsApp” button that opens a chat with: “Hi, I'd like to ask about availability in March.”

3. Make the text easy to read on a phone

Text that is too small, too light or too dense makes visitors tire quickly, especially outdoors in bright light. A few habits help:

  • Use short paragraphs and clear headings, so the page can be scanned.
  • Choose text and background colours with enough contrast. The WCAG accessibility guideline asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text, and 3:1 for large text.
  • Make links and buttons large enough to tap without hitting the wrong one.
  • Put the most important information first, not after a long introduction.

4. Use images that explain, and prepare them properly

Photos should help a customer understand what they would be buying: the room, the equipment, the finished job. Decorative stock images rarely do that. Preparing images properly matters as much as choosing them.

  • Resize photos to the size they will be shown, instead of uploading a camera original.
  • Compress them and use a modern format where possible, so the page opens sooner on mobile data.
  • Crop on purpose, so the subject is visible on a narrow screen.
  • Write a short, honest description for each informative image, for people who cannot see it.

Google recommends that the main content of a page appear within 2.5 seconds, measured as Largest Contentful Paint at the 75th percentile of page loads. That is a recommended target, not a guarantee of more enquiries, but oversized images are a common reason pages miss it.

5. Reduce friction in forms

Every extra field is another reason to give up. Ask only what you need to reply, and let people choose how to reach you.

  • Ask for a name, a way to contact them and what they want. Everything else can wait for the conversation.
  • Require one contact method, not three.
  • Use visible labels and clear error messages that say what to fix.
  • Tell people what happens next, and offer WhatsApp as an alternative to the form.
  • Test it. A form that fails silently is worse than no form.

Illustration only: fictional form

Before

Twelve required fields, including postal address and a long “how did you hear about us” list. The send button is below the fold.

After

Name, WhatsApp or phone number, and one box: “What would you like to ask?” A WhatsApp button sits beside it.

Measure the thing that matters

Button taps are a start, but they do not show how many conversations began, or how many ended in work. A simple record is often enough. For each enquiry, note the date, how the person found you, how they contacted you, what they asked about, how long you took to reply, and what happened in the end. After a few weeks you can see which improvements are worth more of your time.

If you set up analytics, do it deliberately: decide what you want to know, tell visitors what you collect, and remember that a conversation inside WhatsApp will not appear in your website's numbers. Asking new customers “how did you find us?” costs nothing and tells you a lot.
